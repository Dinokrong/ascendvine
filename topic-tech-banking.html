Technology Banking

The Technology sector is often the largest and most active coverage group at major investment banks, covering everything from semiconductor hardware and foundries to SaaS software and internet platforms. TMT offers exposure to household names and fast-paced innovation, with unique valuation frameworks driven by growth, recurring revenue, and the semiconductor cycle.

Basic — Hardware & Semiconductors: The Value Chain

The Technology Hardware & Semiconductors sector forms the physical backbone of the digital economy. It presents unique challenges: high capital intensity, deep technological specialization, and extreme cyclicality.

Intellectual Property (IP) & Core Design

  • Business Model: Licensing fees and royalties.
  • Economics: Extremely high margins (>90% gross margin), low capital expenditure (CapEx).
  • Example: ARM Holdings.

Design (Fabless)

  • Business Model: Sales of physical chips (units × ASP).
  • Economics: High R&D expense, low CapEx, generally high gross margins (>50-60%).
  • Examples: NVIDIA, AMD, Qualcomm, Broadcom.

Manufacturing (Foundry)

  • Business Model: Manufacturing service fees (wafer sales).
  • Economics: Massive CapEx intensity (building a leading-edge fab costs >$20B), high barriers to entry, volume-driven.
  • Examples: TSMC, GlobalFoundries, UMC.

Integrated Device Manufacturers (IDM)

  • Business Model: Sales of physical chips.
  • Economics: High R&D and high CapEx. They capture the full margin stack but bear the risk of filling their own factories.
  • Examples: Intel, Samsung Electronics, Texas Instruments, Micron.

Semiconductor Capital Equipment ("Semicap")

  • Business Model: Selling equipment systems + recurring service/maintenance contracts.
  • Economics: High technical moats, cyclical revenues tied to foundry CapEx budgets.
  • Examples: ASML, Applied Materials, Lam Research, KLA.

Key Operational Metrics: Hardware

Book-to-Bill Ratio

  • Ratio > 1.0: Demand is outstripping supply. The company is building backlog. Suggests growth.
  • Ratio < 1.0: Supply is exceeding demand. The company is burning through backlog. Suggests slowing demand.
💡 Key Insight: A Book-to-Bill ratio that is "too high" (e.g., > 1.5) can be a warning sign of "double ordering," where customers panic and order twice what they need, leading to an eventual inventory glut when demand normalizes.

Average Selling Price (ASP)

  • Calculated as Total Revenue / Total Units Shipped.
  • Hardware trends are generally deflationary: Technology improves, and older tech becomes cheaper. Companies must continuously release new products to maintain or grow ASPs.
  • Mix Shift: Moving customers to higher-end products (e.g., selling a "Pro" model instead of a base model) drives ASP growth without needing to sell more units.

Industry Dynamics & Cyclicality

The Cycle Mechanism

  • Rising Demand: New technology (e.g., AI, 5G, Cloud) drives demand for chips.
  • Shortage: Capacity is fixed in the short term. Prices and lead times increase. Margins expand.
  • CapEx Response: Foundries and IDMs announce massive spending to build new fabs (which take 2-3 years to come online).
  • Oversupply (Glut): New capacity comes online just as demand normalizes or macroeconomy cools.
  • Correction: Prices crash. Inventory is written down, utilization drops.
  • Consolidation: Weak players fail or cut spending; supply tightens, resetting the cycle.

Moore's Law

The observation that the number of transistors on a microchip doubles approximately every two years, while the cost of computers is halved.

  • Economic Implication: Companies must run fast just to stay in place. If you don't transition to the next "node" (generation of manufacturing process), your product becomes obsolete and uncompetitive on cost.

Valuation: Hardware & Semiconductors

Cycle-Adjusted Multiples

  • At the Cycle Peak: Earnings are artificially high. A stock might trade at a low P/E (e.g., 8x) because investors expect earnings to fall next year. This is a "value trap."
  • At the Cycle Trough: Earnings are depressed. A stock might trade at a high P/E (e.g., 40x or even negative) because investors are pricing in a recovery.

Advanced — Cyclical Normalization (Hardware)

The P/E Paradox

  • Peak Cycle: Investors anticipate earnings cuts. The "E" (denominator) is inflated by peak pricing and utilization. The stock price (numerator) falls in anticipation. Result: Low P/E (e.g., 8x-12x).
  • Trough Cycle: Investors anticipate a recovery. The "E" is depressed due to low utilization and inventory writedowns. The stock price recovers early. Result: High P/E (e.g., 25x-30x).
💡 Interview Tip: If asked "Would you buy a memory chip company trading at 6x P/E?", the sophisticated answer is not "Yes, it's cheap," but "We need to check where we are in the cycle. 6x often signals peak earnings, not a bargain. I would look at P/Book and historical trough multiples instead."

Through-Cycle Analysis

  • Average Margins: Calculate the average operating margin over the last full cycle (typically 3-5 years) and apply it to current or forward revenue.
  • Trough Valuation: Historical support levels often form around specific multiples of Book Value (P/B) rather than earnings, especially for capital-intensive memory (DRAM/NAND) companies.
💡 Key Insight: For memory companies (Micron, SK Hynix), Price-to-Book (P/B) is often a more reliable valuation anchor than P/E. A P/B of 0.8x-1.0x historically signals a buying opportunity (trough), while a P/B of 3x-4x signals a peak.

Analyzing the Semiconductor Cycle: The "Bullwhip Effect"

  • Shortage Phase: Demand exceeds supply. Lead times extend (e.g., from 12 weeks to 50 weeks).
  • Double Ordering: Panic buying sets in. Customers place orders with multiple distributors or order 2× what they need to ensure they get enough allocation.
  • The Glut: Supply eventually catches up as capacity expands. Demand softens or double-ordering is revealed. Customers cancel orders abruptly.
  • Inventory Correction: Utilization crashes to burn off excess channel inventory.

Key Metrics to Watch

  • Book-to-Bill Ratio: A ratio > 1.0 implies expansion; < 1.0 implies contraction.
  • Days of Inventory (DOI): Track inventory on the company's balance sheet and channel inventory (at distributors). Rising DOI while revenue growth slows is a classic sell signal.
  • Lead Times: When lead times start to shrink from record highs, pricing power usually erodes shortly after.

Fab Economics: The IDM vs. Foundry Model

Why the Foundry Model Won (Mostly)

  • CapEx Intensity: A leading-edge logic fab costs >$20 billion. Keeping a fab full requires massive scale across diverse products. IDMs focused on a single product category struggle to fill this capacity, resulting in chronically low utilization.
  • Ecosystem Alignment: Foundries are "customer agnostic," allowing them to aggregate demand from competitors (e.g., Apple, Qualcomm, and NVIDIA all filling TSMC's fabs).

Yield Curves

  • Mature Node: Yields are typically >90%.
  • Ramp Phase: When a new node (e.g., 3nm) launches, yields may be <50%.
  • Economics: Profitability is non-linear. Improving yield from 50% to 80% effectively lowers cost per chip by nearly 40% without additional capex.

Geopolitics and Reshoring

The concentration of leading-edge manufacturing in Taiwan (TSMC) and South Korea (Samsung) represents a single point of failure for the global economy.

  • Governments (US CHIPS Act, EU Chips Act) are subsidizing local fabs. However, structural costs in the US/EU are higher due to labor, construction, and supply chain fragmentation.
  • The "Fab Shell" Strategy: Companies may build the physical structures (shells) for fabs ahead of demand, but only install the expensive tools (equipment) when demand is confirmed. Tools represent ~70-75% of total fab cost.

Basic — Internet & Software Business Models

The Technology sector, specifically Internet and Software, represents one of the most dynamic areas of investment banking coverage. Unlike traditional industries driven by tangible assets and marginal production costs, software businesses exhibit near-zero marginal costs, high recurring revenue, and powerful network effects.

Software as a Service (SaaS)

SaaS is the dominant business model in modern software. Instead of buying software once (a perpetual license), customers pay a subscription fee (monthly or annually) to access software hosted in the cloud.

  • Revenue Quality: Revenue is recurring and generally more predictable than one-time sales.
  • Delivery: Software is hosted centrally (cloud) and accessed via the internet, eliminating the need for customers to manage servers.
💡 Key Insight: The core investment thesis for SaaS is predictability. Because revenue is recurring, investors can model future cash flows with higher confidence, commanding higher valuation multiples.

Consumption-Based (Usage-Based)

Common in infrastructure software (e.g., cloud platforms like AWS, Azure, or data platforms like Snowflake).

  • Model: Customers pay only for what they use (e.g., per gigabyte of storage, per minute of compute time).
  • Dynamics: This aligns costs with value but makes revenue harder to forecast than fixed-fee subscriptions.

Bookings vs. Billings vs. Revenue

  • Bookings (Total Contract Value - TCV): The total value of the signed contract. This is an internal sales metric, not a GAAP financial statement line item.
  • Billings (Cash Collections): The amount actually invoiced to the customer. Formula: Billings = Revenue + Change in Deferred Revenue.
  • Revenue (GAAP): The value of the service delivered in the period. For a $240, 24-month contract, Revenue is recognized ratably at $10 per month.

Key Metrics: The "Alphabet Soup" of TMT

ARR and MRR

  • ARR (Annual Recurring Revenue): The annualized value of recurring subscription contracts. If a customer pays $100/month, the ARR is $1,200.
  • MRR (Monthly Recurring Revenue): The monthly value of recurring revenue.
  • Importance: ARR is often used as a proxy for "top-line" size in valuation, sometimes replacing GAAP Revenue because GAAP revenue recognition can lag behind actual contract wins.

Churn and Retention

  • Customer Churn (Logo Churn): Percentage of customers who cancel.
  • Gross Dollar Retention (GDR): Measures revenue retained from the existing customer base, excluding upsells. It cannot exceed 100%.
  • Net Dollar Retention (NDR): Formula: (Starting ARR - Churn + Expansion) / Starting ARR. Significance: An NDR > 100% means the company can grow even without adding new customers. Top-tier SaaS companies achieve 120%+ NDR.

LTV / CAC (Unit Economics)

  • CAC (Customer Acquisition Cost): Total Sales & Marketing expense divided by the number of new customers acquired.
  • LTV (Lifetime Value): The total contribution profit a customer generates over their life. Formula Proxy: (Average Revenue Per User × Gross Margin %) / Churn Rate.
  • Benchmark: An LTV/CAC ratio of 3:1 or higher is generally considered healthy.

Rule of 40

  • Formula: Revenue Growth Rate % + Profit Margin % (Profit usually defined as Free Cash Flow margin or EBITDA margin).
  • Interpretation: If the sum is > 40%, the company is operating efficiently. A company growing at 50% with -10% margins passes the rule (40%). A company growing at 10% with 20% margins fails (30%).

Consumer Internet & Marketplaces

  • GMV (Gross Merchandise Value): The total dollar value of goods sold through the platform. This is not the company's revenue.
  • Revenue: The company's "take rate" or commission on the GMV. Formula: Revenue = GMV × Take Rate.
  • Business Model: Marketplaces (e.g., Airbnb, Uber) connect buyers and sellers without owning the inventory. This is "asset-light."

The Rise of Generative AI & LLMs

  • Foundation Models: The base layer models (e.g., GPT, Claude, Gemini) that are trained on vast amounts of internet data. These are capital intensive to build (costing $100M+ in compute).
  • Inference: The process of the model generating a response. Unlike traditional software where the marginal cost of a user action is near zero, AI has a real marginal cost (GPU compute time) for every query.
  • Tokens: The currency of LLMs. 1,000 tokens is roughly 750 words. Pricing is often based on "Price per Million Tokens."

Strategic Industry Dynamics

Vertical vs. Horizontal SaaS

  • Horizontal SaaS: Solutions that serve a specific function across all industries (e.g., Salesforce for CRM, Slack for communication, Zoom for video). Pros: Massive TAM (Total Addressable Market). Cons: Intense competition; difficult to differentiate from large platforms (Microsoft, Google) that can bundle similar functionality.
  • Vertical SaaS: Solutions built for a specific industry (e.g., Toast for restaurants, Veeva for pharma). Pros: Lower churn (tailored to workflow), higher pricing power, dominance in niche markets. Cons: Smaller TAM limits scale.

Valuation Methodologies: Software

EV / Revenue (Revenue Multiples)

  • The primary valuation metric for high-growth, unprofitable tech companies is Enterprise Value / Revenue (or EV / ARR).
  • Why not EBITDA?: Many software companies reinvest all profits into Sales & Marketing to acquire customers. Current EBITDA is often negative, making EV/EBITDA meaningless.
  • Forward Metrics: Valuations are typically based on NTM (Next Twelve Months) revenue to account for high growth rates.

Advanced — Growth-Adjusted Multiples (The "SaaS PEG")

Growth-Adjusted Multiple = EV / Forward Revenue / Revenue Growth Rate

If Company A is at 20x Revenue / 100% Growth = 0.2x. If Company B is at 10x Revenue / 20% Growth = 0.5x. A lower Growth-Adjusted Multiple suggests the stock is undervalued relative to its growth rate.

The Rule of 40: Nuances & Trade-offs

  • Metric Selection: Is the margin EBITDA or FCF? EBITDA often excludes stock-based compensation (SBC), which can be a massive real cost for tech firms. FCF is generally preferred by conservative investors.
  • Weighting: Not all "40s" are created equal. A company with 40% growth and 0% margin is usually valued higher than a company with 10% growth and 30% margin. The market pays a premium for the growth component because it implies a larger future earnings base.

EV/ARR vs. EV/Revenue

  • Rapid Growth: ARR usually leads GAAP Revenue. Valuing off GAAP Revenue for a company that just signed massive deals effectively penalizes them for accounting lag.
  • Churn Events: If a major customer churns, ARR drops immediately, but GAAP Revenue recognizes the remaining contract term. In this case, EV/ARR is the more honest forward-looking metric.

Unit Economics Deep Dive

Deconstructing LTV

LTV = ARPA × Gross Margin / Churn Rate

However, this assumes flat behavior forever. In reality, successful software companies have Negative Churn (Net Dollar Retention > 100%). If expansion revenue outpaces churn, the denominator (Churn Rate) is effectively negative, making the theoretical LTV infinite.

Cohort Analysis (Layer Cake Charts)

  • The "Layer Cake" chart stacks revenue from customer cohorts (e.g., "2020 Vintage", "2021 Vintage") on top of each other.
  • Segment-Based Cohorts: Tracking by plan type (Enterprise vs. SMB). This often reveals that SMB churn is high (20%+) while Enterprise churn is low (5%), masking the aggregate churn number.
  • Unhealthy Cohort: The bar shrinking significantly forces the company to sell an ever-increasing amount of new business just to replace lost revenue (the "leaky bucket").

Advanced Operational Metrics

Net Dollar Retention (NDR) Decomposition

  • Company A: 5% Churn + 15% Expansion = 110% NDR. (Healthy, stable).
  • Company B: 20% Churn + 30% Expansion = 110% NDR. (High risk — if expansion slows, the high churn will collapse the business).

The Magic Number

Magic Number = (Current Qtr Revenue — Previous Qtr Revenue) × 4 / Previous Qtr Sales & Marketing Expense

  • > 0.75: Highly efficient. Pour more fuel on the fire.
  • ≤ 0.75: Inefficient. Something is wrong with product-market fit or sales execution. Fix the funnel before spending more.

The Burn Multiple

  • ≤ 1.0x: Excellent (burning $1 to add $1 of recurring revenue).
  • > 2.0x: Suspect (burning $2 to add $1 of recurring revenue).
  • > 3.0x: Dangerous (unsustainable unless very early stage).

Software M&A Considerations

Deferred Revenue Write-Down (The "Haircut")

  • When a software company is acquired, its Balance Sheet is reset to fair value. Deferred Revenue (cash collected for future service) is often written down significantly because the acquirer only recognizes the "cost to fulfill" the remaining obligation, not the full face value.
  • Impact: Post-acquisition GAAP revenue appears artificially low in the first year.
  • Solution: Analysts look at Non-GAAP Revenue or add back the write-down to assess true operating performance.

Revenue Synergies vs. Cost Synergies

  • Strategic Buyers (e.g., Salesforce, Adobe) focus on Revenue Synergies: They justify high premiums by cross-selling the target's product to their massive existing customer base.
  • Financial Buyers (e.g., Thoma Bravo, Vista) focus on Cost Synergies. They implement operational playbooks: reduced G&A, offshoring R&D, and optimizing pricing.

Deep Dive: Generative AI & The New Tech Stack

Cost Structure Shift: Inference Costs

  • Gross Margin Pressure: Inference is a COGS (Cost of Goods Sold) item. AI-heavy applications may see Gross Margins compress from the standard SaaS 80%+ to 60-70% due to payments to foundation model providers (e.g., OpenAI API fees) and GPU infrastructure costs.
  • Pricing Model: This pushes companies toward usage-based pricing (per token/credit) rather than flat seat-based pricing, to protect margins against heavy users.

The "Wrapper" Risk vs. True Moats

A "Wrapper" is a startup that simply builds a UI on top of an LLM like GPT/Claude/Gemini. These have low barriers to entry and high obsolescence risk.

  • Proprietary Data: The model is fine-tuned on data no one else has (e.g., a legal firm's 50 years of case files).
  • Workflow Integration: The AI is embedded so deeply into a complex workflow that switching is painful.
  • Feedback Loops: The product gets better the more users use it (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback — RLHF), creating a data network effect.

Private vs. Public Market Dynamics

Structured Terms in Late-Stage Private Tech

  • Liquidation Preference: Investors get their money back (1× or more) before employees/founders see a dime.
  • Ratchets: If the IPO price is lower than the private round price, the investor gets extra shares to maintain their returns.
  • Participating Preferred: "Double dipping" — investor gets their money back plus their % share of the remaining upside.
💡 Common Mistake: Assuming a private company valued at $1B is worth the same as a public company with a $1B market cap. The private valuation often includes these structural protections that inflate the "headline" valuation but are worth far less to common shareholders in a downside scenario.
