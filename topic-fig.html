Financial Institutions Group (FIG)

The Financial Institutions Group (FIG) sector is unique because the companies within it do not just use money to facilitate business — money is the business. Unlike an industrial company where debt is a financing choice, for a financial institution, debt (deposits, policyholder liabilities) is a "raw material" used to generate returns. To master FIG valuation and analysis, you must abandon standard metrics like Enterprise Value and EBITDA. Instead, you will focus on Equity Value, Book Value multiples, and dividend-based valuation methodologies.

Basic — The FIG Landscape

  • Commercial Banks: Companies like JPMorgan Chase or Wells Fargo. They accept deposits (liabilities) and lend money (assets). They make money on the "spread" between interest rates.
  • Insurance: Companies like MetLife (Life) or Travelers (P&C). They collect premiums upfront and pay claims later. They make money on underwriting profit and investment income from the "float."
  • Asset & Wealth Management: Companies like BlackRock or Blackstone. They manage client money for a fee. They are capital-light and often valued like standard service businesses.
  • Fintech & Specialty Finance: Companies like PayPal, Visa, or Affirm. These range from payment processors (toll-road models) to digital lenders (balance sheet models).
💡 Interview Tip: Never apply a "one size fits all" approach to FIG. If asked to value a "financial company," immediately ask for clarification: "Are we talking about a balance-sheet intensive business like a Bank or Insurer, or a Capital-light business like an Asset Manager?"

Commercial Banking: The Balance Sheet as a Business

The Business Model

  • Liabilities (Sources of Funds): Deposits (Checking/Savings accounts) are the primary source. These are liabilities because the bank owes the money back to the customer.
  • Assets (Uses of Funds): Loans (Mortgages, Commercial Loans) and Securities (Treasury Bonds). These are assets because they generate cash flow for the bank.

The Income Statement Structure

  • Interest Income: Revenue from loans and securities.
  • Interest Expense: Cost of paying depositors and debt holders.
  • Net Interest Income (NII): The difference (Spread).
  • Provision for Credit Losses (PCL): An expense recorded now for loans expected to go bad in the future.
  • Non-Interest Income: Fees from wealth management, investment banking, or credit cards.
  • Non-interest Expense: Salaries, rent, technology, and overhead (the "Efficiency Ratio" components).

Key Metrics

  • Net Interest Margin (NIM): (Interest Income - Interest Expense) / Average Earning Assets. This measures the raw profitability of the lending engine.
  • Efficiency Ratio: Non-Interest Expense / Total Revenue. Unlike a profit margin, lower is better. It measures how many cents it costs to generate $1 of revenue. Best-in-class banks operate below 50%.
  • Return on Tangible Common Equity (ROTCE): Net Income / (Tangible Common Equity). This is the most correlated metric to valuation. It measures return on the shareholder's "hard" capital.

Impact of Interest Rates & Yield Curve

  • Normal Yield Curve: Long-term rates are higher than short-term rates. Banks profit because the spread is wide.
  • Inverted Yield Curve: Short-term rates are higher than long-term rates. This crushes bank profitability because their funding costs (short-term) rise while their asset yields (long-term) stay flat or fall.

Insurance: Managing Risk and Float

Life vs. Property & Casualty (P&C)

  • P&C (Auto, Home): Short-tail business. Policies are 6-12 months. Claims are binary (accident or no accident). Focus is on underwriting discipline.
  • Life (Term Life, Annuities): Long-tail business. Policies last 20-40 years. The company holds the money for decades before paying out. Focus is on investment returns and actuarial assumptions (mortality rates).

The Concept of "Float"

Because premiums are paid upfront and claims are paid later, insurers hold massive amounts of cash called Float. They invest this float in bonds and stocks. Investment Income is often the primary driver of profit.

Key Metrics

  • Loss Ratio: Claims Paid / Premiums.
  • Expense Ratio: Operating Costs / Premiums.
  • Combined Ratio: Loss Ratio + Expense Ratio. <100%: Underwriting Profit. >100%: Underwriting Loss (relies on investment income to be profitable).

Asset Management: A Fee-Based Model

  • Management Fees: A recurring % of Assets Under Management (AUM). This is the stable "annuity" stream.
  • Performance Fees (Carried Interest): A share of the profits (e.g., 20%) if returns exceed a hurdle rate. This is common in Hedge Funds and Private Equity but volatile.
  • Multiples: P/E is the standard. EV/EBITDA is also used since interest expense is not an operating item for them.
  • AUM Multiples: Enterprise Value / AUM. A rule of thumb (e.g., 2% of AUM) used for sanity checks.

Fintech & Specialty Finance

  • Payments (Visa, Stripe): They take a tiny cut (Take Rate) of every transaction volume (TPV). They take no credit risk. Valuation: EV/Sales, EV/EBITDA. Key metric: Total Payment Volume (TPV).
  • Digital Lenders (SoFi, Affirm): They use an app to originate loans. If they hold loans on the balance sheet, they face credit risk. Valuation: P/TBV. Key metric: Delinquency Rate.
  • Neobanks (Chime, Revolut): Digital-only banking interfaces. Most rely on Interchange Fees (swiping debit cards) rather than Net Interest Income.

Valuation Methodology: Why DDM?

Why Unlevered DCF Fails for Banks

  • Debt is Inventory: In a DCF, we strip out debt to get to Enterprise Value. But for a bank, "debt" (deposits) is the raw material needed to create "products" (loans). You cannot separate operations from financing.
  • Interest is COGS: Interest expense is a bank's Cost of Goods Sold. Adding it back to get EBITDA or Unlevered FCF makes no sense.
  • CapEx is Irrelevant: A bank's reinvestment isn't buying new factories; it's growing the loan book, which requires retaining capital.

The Dividend Discount Model (DDM)

  • Step 1: Forecast Net Income.
  • Step 2: Calculate required capital to support asset growth.
  • Step 3: Dividends = Net Income - Capital Retained for Growth.
  • Step 4: Discount Dividends and Terminal Value by the Cost of Equity (Ke).
💡 Relationship between P/TBV and ROTCE: P/TBV = (ROTCE - g) / (Cost of Equity - g). This formula explains why banks with higher Returns on Tangible Equity trade at higher Price-to-Book multiples. If a bank's ROTCE equals its Cost of Equity, it should theoretically trade at 1.0× Book Value.

Regulatory Capital: The "Buffer"

  • CET1 (Common Equity Tier 1): The highest quality capital (Common Stock + Retained Earnings).
  • Risk-Weighted Assets (RWA): Not all assets are equal. Cash has 0% risk. A mortgage might have 50% risk. A corporate loan might have 100% risk.
  • The Ratio: CET1 Ratio = CET1 Capital / RWA.
  • The Rule: Regulators (like the Fed) enforce a minimum ratio (e.g., 4.5% + buffers). If a bank drops below this, it is seized.

Advanced — Valuation Frameworks

The Residual Income Model (RIM)

The core philosophy of RIM is that a bank is worth its Current Book Value plus the present value of the Excess Returns it generates in the future.

  • Excess Return: (ROE - Cost of Equity) × Beginning Book Value.
  • The Formula: Value = Current Book Value + Σ [(ROE_t - Ke) × BV_{t-1}] / (1 + Ke)^t
💡 Key Insight: This model mathematically proves that Value Creation only happens when ROE > Ke. If a bank's ROE is 10% and its Cost of Equity is 10%, the second term in the formula becomes zero. The bank is worth exactly its Book Value. This explains why P/B multiples track ROE so closely.

Levered DCF for Banks

  • Concept: Instead of projecting Unlevered FCF (to Firm), you project Levered FCF (to Equity) directly.
  • FCFE = Net Income — (Δ Risk-Weighted Assets × Target Tier 1 Ratio).
  • Discount Rate: Since this is cash flow to equity, you must discount at the Cost of Equity (Ke), not WACC.

Advanced Bank Analysis: Liquidity & Stability

Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR)

  • Purpose: Ensures the bank survives a 30-day "run on the bank."
  • Formula: High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) / Total Net Cash Outflows over 30 days.
  • Requirement: Must be ≥ 100%.
  • Implication: Banks must hold low-yielding Treasuries and Cash (HQLA) instead of high-yielding loans, which drags down ROE but ensures survival.

Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR)

  • Purpose: Ensures long-term assets are funded by long-term liabilities.
  • Formula: Available Stable Funding (ASF) / Required Stable Funding (RSF).
  • Requirement: Must be ≥ 100%.
  • Implication: Banks cannot fund 30-year mortgages entirely with volatile overnight wholesale funding. They need "sticky" deposits or long-term debt.

Life Insurance Valuation: Embedded Value

For Life insurers, GAAP accounting is flawed. It recognizes expenses immediately (acquisition costs) but revenue over 30 years. This mismatch hides the true economic value of the business.

Embedded Value = Adjusted Net Worth (ANW) + Value of In-Force Business (VIF)

  • Adjusted Net Worth (ANW): The market value of the insurer's tangible equity (Assets marked to market - Liabilities).
  • Value of In-Force (VIF): The Present Value of future profits from existing policies only.

Fintech Deep Dive: Unit Economics & Risks

Neobanks & Interchange Dependency

  • Revenue Model: They rely on Interchange (swiping fees). They don't lend much, so they miss out on Net Interest Income (the biggest revenue pool in banking).
  • Profitability Challenge: To be profitable, they need users to make them their primary bank (direct deposit) to drive transaction volume.

Payment for Order Flow (PFOF)

  • The Mechanism: Trading apps like Robinhood sell their customer's buy/sell orders to high-frequency trading firms (Market Makers like Citadel). The Market Maker executes the trade and pays Robinhood a tiny fee for the volume.
  • Risk: This creates a conflict of interest (best execution vs. highest payment) and is under intense regulatory scrutiny.

Buy Now, Pay Later (BNPL)

  • The Debate: BNPL firms (Affirm, Klarna) frame themselves as payment facilitators for merchants. However, they take Credit Risk (the customer might not pay the installments).
  • Valuation Impact: If valued as "Payments," they get 20x Revenue multiples. If valued as "Unsecured Consumer Lending," they get 8x Earnings multiples.

Bank M&A: The Technicals

The "Double Mark" of the Balance Sheet

  • Credit Mark: Writing down the loan portfolio for expected lifetime losses. This replaces the target's existing loan reserves.
  • Non-Accretable Difference: The portion of the write-down representing expected losses.
  • Accretable Yield: The portion representing interest rate differences, which is slowly recognized as income over time (accreted).

Core Deposit Intangibles (CDI)

  • Definition: An intangible asset representing the value of the acquired bank's sticky, low-cost checking accounts.
  • Calculation: Often 2-4% of the Core Deposit base.
  • Impact: It boosts assets initially but creates an amortization expense that hurts GAAP Net Income for years (though cash flow remains unaffected).

Synergies: Cost vs. Revenue

  • Cost Synergies: Highly credible. Closing overlapping branches, firing back-office staff. Usually 30-40% of the target's expense base.
  • Revenue Synergies: Skeptically viewed. "Cross-selling" products rarely works as well as planned. Analysts often model 0% revenue synergies to be conservative.

Insurance: Statutory Accounting Nuances

Statutory Strain

  • GAAP: Capitalizes commission (Deferred Acquisition Cost — DAC) and amortizes it over 30 years. Profits look smooth.
  • STAT: Expenses the commission immediately. This creates a massive loss in Year 1 called Statutory Strain, reducing the insurer's surplus capital.
  • Consequence: Rapid growth drains statutory capital. A fast-growing insurer may need to raise capital even if it looks profitable on a GAAP basis.

Loss Triangles & Reserve Development

  • Loss Triangle: A table showing claims for each "Accident Year" as they develop over time.
  • Adverse Development: If an insurer consistently raises its loss estimates for past years, it suggests poor underwriting or aggressive accounting. This kills the stock price.
