Healthcare Group

Healthcare is one of the largest and most complex sectors in investment banking coverage. It is unique because it combines highly technical scientific risks (e.g., will this drug work?) with complex regulatory environments and multiple payer systems.

Basic — Healthcare Subsectors

BioPharmaceuticals (Pharma & Biotech)

  • Big Pharma: Large, diversified companies with significant revenue streams, commercialized products, and massive marketing infrastructures. They often grow by acquiring smaller biotechs.
  • Biotechnology: Historically defined by biological manufacturing processes, but in finance, "Biotech" often refers to smaller, development-stage companies. Many are pre-revenue and cash-burning, relying on equity raises for survival.
  • Specialty Pharma: Companies that focus on specific therapeutic areas (e.g., dermatology, pain management) or complex drug delivery systems.
  • Generics & Biosimilars: Companies that manufacture off-patent drugs. Their business model is volume-driven with lower margins compared to branded innovators.

Medical Devices & Technology (MedTech)

  • Capital Equipment: Expensive machinery (e.g., MRI machines, robotic surgery systems). Sales are "lumpy" and dependent on hospital capital expenditure (CapEx) budgets.
  • Consumables/Implants: Products used once or permanently implanted (e.g., syringes, heart valves, orthopedic knees). These offer more recurring revenue streams.

Healthcare Services (Providers)

  • Facilities: Hospitals, Ambulatory Surgery Centers (ASCs), nursing homes.
  • Laboratories: Diagnostic labs (e.g., blood testing) and Contract Research Organizations (CROs) that run clinical trials for pharma companies.
  • Care Continuum: It is vital to distinguish between Acute Care (short-term treatment for severe injury/illness, e.g., ERs, ICUs) and Post-Acute Care (longer-term recovery, e.g., skilled nursing facilities, rehab centers).

Managed Care & Payers

  • Managed Care Organizations (MCOs): Health insurance companies. Their model is based on collecting premiums and managing medical costs (claims).
  • Medicare: Federal program for the elderly (65+).
  • Medicaid: State-federal program for low-income individuals.
  • Managed Medicare/Medicaid: When the government contracts private MCOs to administer these benefits.

Healthcare IT (HCIT) & Distribution

  • HCIT: Software for electronic medical records (EMR), telemedicine, and revenue cycle management. These are often valued like software/SaaS companies.
  • Distributors & PBMs: Intermediaries that move drugs and money through the system. Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs) negotiate drug prices on behalf of insurers.

Business Models

The Drug Development Lifecycle

  • Pre-Clinical: Lab and animal testing to determine safety before human trials.
  • Phase I: Safety focus. Small number of healthy volunteers (20-80).
  • Phase II: Efficacy proof-of-concept. Mid-sized patient group (dozens to hundreds).
  • Phase III: Large-scale statistical significance. Compares drug to placebo or standard of care (hundreds to thousands).
  • Regulatory Review: Submission of an NDA (New Drug Application) to the FDA.
  • Commercialization: Marketing and sales after approval.
  • Phase IV: Post-marketing surveillance to monitor long-term safety.

The "Patent Cliff" and Generic Entry

  • Paragraph III Filing: A generic company waits for the patent to expire before launching.
  • Paragraph IV Filing: A generic company challenges the patent's validity before it expires. If successful, they may get 180 days of market exclusivity, a massive value driver for generic companies.

Razor / Razorblade Model

Common in MedTech and Life Sciences Tools. A company sells a piece of equipment (the razor) at a lower margin or one-time cost, but requires the customer to buy proprietary consumables (the blades) continuously at high margins.

Key Metrics for Healthcare

Probability of Success (PoS)

In Biopharma, you cannot project revenue without adjusting for risk. PoS is the percentage likelihood that a drug in a specific phase will eventually reach approval.

  • Phase I: Low PoS (e.g., 10%).
  • Phase III: Higher PoS (e.g., 60%).

Peak Sales

The maximum annual revenue a drug is expected to generate during its patent life. This is the single most important input in a drug valuation model.

Medical Loss Ratio (MLR)

Specific to Managed Care (Insurance). It measures the percentage of premium dollars spent on medical claims.

  • MLR = Medical Claims Expense / Premium Revenue.
  • A lower MLR is generally better for the insurer's profitability, though regulations often cap how low it can go (e.g., 80-85%).

Same-Facility (or Same-Store) Growth

Used for Hospitals and Labs. It measures revenue growth from existing facilities, excluding the impact of new openings or acquisitions.

Valuation Considerations

Risk-Adjusted NPV (rNPV)

Standard DCFs fail for pre-revenue biotech because cash flows are negative for years and then potentially massive only if the drug works.

  • In rNPV, you model the cash flows of a drug if it succeeds.
  • You then multiply those cash flows by the cumulative Probability of Success (PoS).
  • This "expected value" is then discounted back to today.
💡 Key Insight: rNPV is mathematically similar to a decision tree analysis. It explicitly accounts for the fact that the drug might fail and generate zero revenue.

Industry Dynamics

The Payer Landscape: HMOs, PPOs, and Government

  • HMO (Health Maintenance Organization): Restrictive network. Patients must see a Primary Care Physician (PCP) first to get a referral. Lower premiums, strict cost control.
  • PPO (Preferred Provider Organization): Flexible network. Patients can see out-of-network doctors for a higher fee. Higher premiums.
  • Medicare Advantage: Private plans (like HMOs/PPOs) that replace traditional government Medicare.

Generic and Biosimilar Erosion

  • Small Molecule Drugs: Generics are chemically identical. Prices crash rapidly (90% drop).
  • Biologics (Large Molecule): Copies are called Biosimilars. They are "highly similar" but not identical due to complex manufacturing. Price erosion is slower and less severe than traditional generics because physicians are reluctant to switch stable patients.

Advanced — Biotech Valuation: rNPV and Pipeline Modeling

The rNPV model differs from a standard DCF in one critical way: cash flows are multiplied by a Probability of Success (POS) factor before being discounted. The POS represents the cumulative likelihood of the drug reaching that stage of cash flow.

💡 Key Insight: Do not double-count risk. If you probability-adjust the cash flows, you should use a discount rate that reflects the commercial risk of a successful product (typically lower than the cost of capital used for a binary-outcome, early-stage drug).

Probability of Success (POS)

  • Phase I: <10% cumulative probability of approval.
  • Phase II: 15-20% cumulative probability.
  • Phase III: 50-60% cumulative probability.
  • NDA/BLA Filed: 85-90% cumulative probability.

Peak Sales Estimation (The Epidemiology Model)

  • Target Population: Total population × Disease Prevalence/Incidence.
  • Addressable Population: Target Population × Diagnosis Rate × Treatment Rate.
  • Target Market: Addressable Population × Eligibility for specific drug class.
  • Market Share: Target Market × Penetration Rate (adoption curve).
  • Net Price: Annual Gross Price × (1 - Gross-to-Net discount).
  • Revenue: Patients × Net Price.
💡 Interview Tip: When asked how to project revenue for a drug, never say "grow it by 10%." Walk through the "Epi Model": Population → Prevalence → Diagnosis → Treatment → Market Share → Price.

Lifecycle Management and Loss of Exclusivity (LoE)

The Patent Cliff vs. Tail Erosion

  • Small Molecules (Chemicals): Erosion Profile: Rapid. Revenue can drop 80-90% within months of the first generic launch. This is often modeled as a "cliff."
  • Biologics (Large Proteins): Erosion Profile: Gradual. Prices may erode 20-40% over several years, with volume stickiness due to physician brand loyalty. This is modeled as a "tail."

Lifecycle Management Strategies

  • New Formulations: Switching from twice-daily pills to once-daily extended release (often protecting the franchise with new patents).
  • New Indications: Gaining approval for the same drug in a different disease.
  • Pediatric Exclusivity: Testing in children to gain an additional 6 months of exclusivity (in certain jurisdictions).

Healthcare Services: Unit Economics and Reimbursement

Hospital Revenue Build: The "Equivalent Admissions" Model

  • Adjusted Admissions (or Equivalent Admissions): Formula: Admissions × (Total Gross Revenue / Inpatient Gross Revenue). This creates a single volume metric that captures the entire facility's activity (both inpatient and outpatient).
  • Revenue per Equivalent Admission: Formula: Total Net Patient Revenue / Equivalent Admissions. Forecast: You project the growth of Equivalent Admissions (Volume) and Rev/Admission (Rate/Price) separately, then multiply.

Payor Mix and "Case Mix Index"

  • Commercial Payors: Typically pay 150-250% of Medicare rates. These are the profit drivers.
  • Government Payors (Medicare/Medicaid): Often cover costs or operate at a slight loss.
  • Case Mix Index (CMI): Measures the acuity (severity) of patients treated. A higher CMI means sicker patients, which justifies higher reimbursement rates but requires higher staffing costs.

M&A Deal Structuring: Bridging the Gap

Contingent Value Rights (CVRs)

A CVR gives shareholders the right to additional cash payments if specific future milestones are met. Why use it?: The buyer believes a drug has a 40% chance of success; the seller believes it has 80%. They cannot agree on a fixed price. A CVR solves this: "I'll pay you $X now, and $Y more if and only if the drug reaches Phase III or gets FDA approval."

Milestones and Royalties ("Biobucks")

  • Upfront Payment: Cash paid at closing (guaranteed).
  • Development Milestones: Payments upon clinical success (e.g., "Start of Phase III").
  • Commercial Milestones: Payments upon hitting revenue targets.
  • Royalties: Percentage of net sales paid to the innovator (typically mid-single digits to low-twenties percent).
