Natural Resources & Energy Group

The Natural Resources and Energy sectors are distinct from other industries because companies in these sectors generally deplete their core assets over time. Unlike a software company that can scale indefinitely, a mine or an oil well runs out. This fundamental difference shapes nearly every aspect of valuation and analysis — from using Net Asset Value (NAV) instead of terminal value DCFs, to specialized metrics like BOE, reserve classifications, and commodity cost curves.

Basic — Energy, Power & Renewables: The Oil & Gas Value Chain

The Energy, Power, and Renewables (EPR) sector is one of the largest and most capital-intensive verticals in investment banking. The Oil & Gas industry is typically segmented into three streams: Upstream, Midstream, and Downstream. Understanding where a company sits in this value chain is critical because it dictates the business model, risk profile, and valuation methodology.

Upstream (Exploration & Production or "E&P")

  • Business Model: High risk, high reward. Revenue is directly tied to commodity prices (the price of oil/gas) and production volumes.
  • Key Asset: Subsurface reserves (oil and gas in the ground).
  • Primary Risk: Commodity price risk and geological risk (dry holes).

Midstream

  • Business Model: Fee-based revenues. They charge producers a fee to move volume through pipelines or store it in tanks.
  • Risk Profile: Lower risk than upstream. Revenues are often protected by long-term "take-or-pay" contracts, meaning the customer pays even if they don't ship the volume.
  • Corporate Structure: Historically often structured as Master Limited Partnerships (MLPs) in the US to benefit from tax advantages.

Downstream

  • Business Model: Margin-based. They profit from the "crack spread" — the difference between the price of crude oil (input) and the price of refined products (output).
  • Dynamics: Generally inversely correlated with Upstream. When oil prices fall, Upstream suffers, but Downstream may benefit if input costs drop faster than product prices.

Oilfield Services (OFS)

  • Business Model: B2B services. Their revenue depends on the capital expenditure (CapEx) budgets of E&P companies.
  • Cyclicality: Highly cyclical. When oil prices drop, E&P companies cut CapEx immediately, hurting OFS firms first.
💡 Interview Tip: "Integrated Majors" (like ExxonMobil, Shell, BP) own assets across the entire value chain (Upstream + Midstream + Downstream). This vertical integration provides a natural hedge against commodity price swings.

Power, Utilities & Renewables

Regulated Utilities

  • Business Model: "Cost-of-Service" or "Rate Base" model. The government allows them to charge rates that cover their operating costs plus a guaranteed Return on Equity (ROE) on their capital investments (Rate Base).
  • Valuation: Viewed as "bond proxies" because they offer stable dividends and low risk. Valuations often focus on P/E or EV/Rate Base.
  • Growth: Growth comes from "gold-plating" — investing more capital into infrastructure to increase the Rate Base, thereby increasing the absolute dollar amount of allowed profit.

Independent Power Producers (IPPs) / Merchant Power

  • Business Model: They are price takers. They sell power at the clearing price of the market, which fluctuates based on supply, demand, and weather.
  • Spark Spread: (Electricity Price) - (Natural Gas Cost / Heat Rate).
  • Dark Spread: (Electricity Price) - (Coal Cost / Heat Rate).
  • Quark Spread: The spread for nuclear generation.
  • Risk: Higher Beta (risk) than utilities. If power prices drop or fuel costs rise, their margins collapse.

Renewables (Wind, Solar, Biofuels, Storage)

  • Solar & Wind: High upfront CapEx, near-zero marginal cost. Revenue is often secured via Power Purchase Agreements (PPAs) — long-term contracts (15-20 years) with utilities or corporations to sell power at a fixed price.
  • Yield (CAFD): For operational assets, investors focus on Cash Available for Distribution (CAFD). This is a proxy for free cash flow available to pay dividends to shareholders.
  • TEV/MW: Valuation per unit of capacity.

Key Operational Metrics: Oil & Gas

  • Barrel of Oil Equivalent (BOE): A way to standardize natural gas and oil into one unit. Rule of Thumb: 6,000 cubic feet (6 Mcf) of natural gas has roughly the energy content of 1 barrel (bbl) of oil. Formula: Total BOE = Bbls of Oil + (Mcf of Gas / 6).
  • 1P (Proved): >90% probability of recovery. The most conservative number, used for lending and US accounting.
  • 2P (Proved + Probable): >50% probability. The most realistic view of what a company will actually produce.
  • 3P (Proved + Probable + Possible): >10% probability. The optimistic view.
  • Reserve Replacement Ratio (RRR): Measures if a company is finding enough new oil to replace what it produced. RRR = (New Reserves Added) / (Production). An RRR < 100% is a warning sign that the company is depleting faster than it's replenishing.

Power Metrics

  • MW (Capacity): The maximum output potential of a plant at a single instant (like the horsepower of a car).
  • MWh (Generation): The actual amount of electricity produced over time (like the miles driven).
  • Capacity Factor: Actual MWh / (Max MW × Hours in Period). Solar: Typically 15-25%. Wind: Typically 30-50%. Nuclear/Gas: Can be >90% (baseload power).
  • Heat Rate: Efficiency metric for thermal plants. How much fuel (MMBtu) is needed to generate 1 kWh of electricity. Lower is better.

Valuation Methodologies: Energy

Net Asset Value (NAV) — Crucial for E&P

For E&P companies, a Discounted Cash Flow (DCF) model is typically run on individual wells or fields rather than the corporate entity, then summed up. This is because reserves deplete; there is no "terminal value" from infinite operations.

  • Project Cash Flows: Forecast revenue (Price × Volume) minus costs (Taxes, Royalties, Opex) for the life of the reserves.
  • Add Value of Undeveloped Acreage: Estimate value for land not yet drilled.
  • Subtract Net Debt: To get to Equity Value.
  • No Perpetuity: Unlike a standard DCF, you do not calculate a terminal value based on infinite growth. The terminal value is zero when the reserves are exhausted.

EV/EBITDAX — Upstream Specific

  • EBITDAX: Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, Depletion, Amortization, and Exploration Expenses.
  • Adding back exploration costs normalizes the metric across companies using different accounting standards.

P/Distributable Cash Flow (P/DCF) — Midstream/MLP/YieldCos

  • Distributable Cash Flow (or CAFD): Cash Flow from Operations minus Maintenance CapEx.
  • Dividend Yield: Often the primary valuation driver. If a YieldCo trades at a 6% yield and acquires an asset with an 8% cash yield, the deal is accretive.

EV/Regulated Asset Base (Rate Base) — Utilities

For regulated utilities, earnings are a function of their "Rate Base" (the asset value approved by regulators). Investors look at the premium paid over the Rate Base. If a utility trades at 1.2× Rate Base, it means the market believes the company will earn returns above its allowed ROE.

Energy Industry Dynamics & Risks

Commodity Price Cycles

The O&G industry is notoriously cyclical. High prices incentivize over-production → Supply exceeds demand → Prices crash → Investment dries up → Supply shortages → Prices rise.

Capital Intensity & Decline Curves

Oil wells follow a "decline curve" — production is highest on day 1 and falls over time (often dropping 50-70% in the first year for shale wells).

  • Implication: E&P companies are on a "treadmill." They must constantly spend CapEx to drill new wells just to keep production flat.

The Energy Transition

  • Terminal Value Risk: Long-term oil assets face "stranded asset" risk if demand collapses due to EV adoption or carbon taxes.
  • Cost of Capital: Hydrocarbon companies generally face a higher Cost of Capital (WACC) due to ESG mandates limiting the pool of available investors.
  • Developer vs. Operator: In renewables, "Developers" (who build projects and sell them) are valued differently from "Operators" (who hold assets for yield). Developers are valued on pipeline growth; Operators are valued on yield/dividend capacity.

Basic — Natural Resources: Mining Subsectors

The Natural Resources sector is distinct because companies deplete their core assets over time. Unlike a software company that can scale indefinitely, a mine runs out. This shapes everything from valuation (NAV instead of terminal value DCF) to management strategy.

Precious Metals (Gold, Silver, PGM)

  • Drivers: Often driven by investment demand, inflation expectations, and currency fluctuations rather than pure industrial supply/demand. Gold is counter-cyclical and viewed as a "safe haven."
  • Characteristics: High value-to-weight ratio, allowing mines to be located in remote areas with higher transport costs.

Base Metals (Copper, Zinc, Nickel, Aluminum)

  • Drivers: Driven by global GDP growth, industrial activity, urbanization, and electrification. These are "cyclical" commodities.
  • Characteristics: Often require massive processing facilities and energy inputs (especially Aluminum).

Bulk Commodities (Iron Ore, Coal, Bauxite)

  • Drivers: Driven heavily by infrastructure spending and steel production (Iron Ore + Met Coal = Steel).
  • Characteristics: Low value-to-weight ratio. Logistics (rail and port access) are often the biggest competitive advantage/bottleneck.

Mining Business Models & Lifecycles

The Resource Lifecycle

  • Exploration (Juniors): Companies searching for deposits. Revenue: None. Funding: Equity issuance. Risk: Extremely high (binary outcomes).
  • Development: Companies constructing the mine or drilling infrastructure. Revenue: None. Funding: Debt (Project Finance) and Equity. Risk: High (construction delays, CAPEX blowouts, permitting).
  • Production (Majors): Operating mines/wells selling commodities. Revenue: Driven by commodity price × volume. Focus: Cost control, free cash flow, dividend payments.
  • Reclamation/Closure: Restoring the land after depletion. This represents a significant long-term liability (Asset Retirement Obligation).

Alternative Models: Royalty & Streaming

  • Royalty Companies: Receive a percentage of revenue (e.g., Net Smelter Return) from a mine. They have no exposure to operating costs.
  • Streaming Companies: Provide an upfront payment to receive the right to purchase a portion of production (usually a byproduct) at a fixed, deeply discounted price in the future.
💡 Key Insight: Royalty and Streaming companies often trade at significantly higher valuation multiples (e.g., 20x+ EBITDA) compared to operators (e.g., 5-8x EBITDA) because they offer "exploration upside" without the operating cost risk.

Key Metrics: Mining

  • Grade: Concentration of metal (g/t or %). Higher grade = lower cost.
  • Recovery Rate: Percentage of metal extracted from ore.
  • Strip Ratio: Waste moved per unit of ore. Lower is better.
  • AISC (All-In Sustaining Cost): Comprehensive cost metric including G&A and sustaining CAPEX.

Oil & Gas Metrics (additional)

  • Reserve Life Index (RLI): Measures how many years of production remain at current rates. Formula: Proved Reserves / Annual Production.
  • Production Replacement Ratio: Formula: New Reserves Added / Annual Production. A ratio > 100% is healthy.
  • Finding & Development (F&D) Cost: The cost to discover and develop one barrel of oil. Formula: Total Exploration & Development CAPEX / Reserve Additions.

Valuation: Net Asset Value (NAV)

NAV is the "Gold Standard" for single-asset mining and E&P companies. A standard DCF assumes a company grows into perpetuity. A mine or oil well runs out. Therefore, we project cash flows for the specific life of the asset.

  • Build a DCF for each individual mine/field (Asset Level).
  • Sum the PV of cash flows from all operating assets.
  • Add Value for non-operating assets (exploration land, stockpiles).
  • Subtract Corporate Adjustments (PV of corporate G&A, net debt, reclamation liabilities).
  • Divide by share count to get NAV/Share.

Mining Industry Dynamics

The Cost Curve

  • Marginal Producer: The highest-cost producer needed to meet global demand sets the floor for the commodity price. If the price drops below their cost, they shut down, restricting supply.
  • Positioning: Investors prefer companies in the bottom quartile of the cost curve. These companies remain profitable even when commodity prices crash.

Commodity Supercycles

Resources are notoriously cyclical. High prices lead to over-investment → Oversupply → Price crash → Under-investment → Shortage → High prices. Banking deal flow often follows this cycle: M&A peaks at high prices (companies buying growth) and distressed activity peaks at low prices (companies selling assets to survive).

Advanced — NAV Modeling & Asset-Level Valuation (Mining)

The "No Terminal Value" Reality

Unlike standard DCF models where 60-70% of value comes from the Terminal Value, a pure mining NAV model implies zero terminal value. The resource is finite.

💡 Key Insight: While the mathematical terminal value is zero, practitioners often model "Resource Conversion" or "Life of Mine (LOM) Extension" as a separate value bucket. This assigns probability-weighted value to the likelihood that exploration success will extend the mine's life.

Differentiated Discount Rates

  • Stage of Development: Producing mines might be discounted at 5-7% (real terms), while development projects (pre-construction) might warrant 10-12% or higher to reflect execution risk.
  • Jurisdictional Risk: A mine in Nevada will have a lower discount rate than a geologically identical mine in a politically unstable region.
  • Commodity Mix: Assets with high exposure to volatile byproducts might carry higher betas.

Mining Economics: Grade, Recovery, and Optimization

Cutoff Grade Optimization

Cutoff Grade = (Process Cost + G&A) / (Price × Recovery)

Strategic Nuance: Miners can "High Grade" (raise the cutoff grade) to maximize near-term cash flow at the expense of mine life. Conversely, they can lower the cutoff grade during high-price environments to extend mine life, accepting lower near-term cash flows.

Recovery Rates & Processing Complexity

  • Refractory Ore: Requires expensive processing (e.g., autoclaves, roasters) due to chemical bonding. This lowers recovery rates or increases CAPEX/OPEX significantly compared to "oxide" ores which can be heap leached cheaply.
  • Sensitivity: A 1% change in recovery rate flows directly to the bottom line (pure margin), making it one of the most sensitive inputs in a NAV model.

Byproduct Credits vs. Co-Product Accounting

  • Byproduct Method: Revenue from secondary metals (e.g., silver found in a gold mine) is deducted from operating costs. This artificially lowers the reported cash cost or AISC of the primary metal.
  • Co-Product Method: Costs are allocated to each metal proportionally to revenue. This is more accurate when the secondary metal is a significant portion of value.

Advanced E&P Valuation: The NAV Model

Working Interest vs. Net Revenue Interest

  • Working Interest (WI): The % of costs the company pays (e.g., 80% of drilling CAPEX).
  • Net Revenue Interest (NRI): The % of revenue the company keeps after paying royalties to landowners.
  • The Gap: NRI is always lower than WI. A company might pay 100% of the cost (100% WI) but only keep 80% of the revenue (80% NRI) because the landowner takes a 20% royalty off the top.

Successful Efforts vs. Full Cost Accounting

  • Successful Efforts (SE): If you drill a "dry hole" (find no oil), you expense the cost immediately on the Income Statement. Only successful wells are capitalized to the Balance Sheet (PP&E). Lower Net Income in years with high exploration activity. Used by most Majors (Exxon, Chevron).
  • Full Cost (FC): ALL exploration costs (even dry holes) are capitalized into the Balance Sheet as part of the asset base. Higher Net Income initially. Massive Balance Sheet. The Ceiling Test: Every quarter, FC companies must prove the PV-10 of their reserves exceeds their capitalized Net Book Value. If prices crash, the PV-10 drops below Book Value, forcing a massive, non-cash write-down (a "ceiling test impairment").

Advanced E&P Metrics

  • Recycle Ratio: Measures the profitability of finding and developing new reserves. Recycle Ratio = (Profit per BOE) / (Finding & Development Cost per BOE). A ratio ≥2.0x is generally considered healthy.
  • Reserve Life Index (RLI): How many years production can be sustained at current rates. RLI = Total Reserves / Annual Production.
  • PV-10: Pre-tax present value of estimated future cash flows from proved reserves, discounted at 10%. Used widely for comparability.
  • Standardized Measure: An SEC-mandated GAAP metric. It is PV-10 after future income taxes.

Advanced E&P Risking (Reserve Risk Factors)

  • PDP (Proved Developed Producing): Typically risked at 100% (no discount) or slightly less. These wells are already flowing.
  • PDNP (Proved Developed Non-Producing): Risked around 80-90%. The well exists but needs minor work or hook-up to flow.
  • PUD (Proved Undeveloped): Risked around 50-70%. These are identified locations that are reasonably certain to be economical but haven't been drilled.
  • Probable/Possible: Risked heavily (10-40%). These are speculative locations.

Midstream & Downstream Economics

Midstream Contract Structures

  • Take-or-Pay: The shipper (customer) pays for a specific capacity reservation regardless of whether they actually transport volumes. This makes cash flows "bond-like" and highly valued.
  • Minimum Volume Commitments (MVCs): Similar to take-or-pay, ensuring a floor on revenue.
  • Fee-Based: Revenue depends on actual throughput volume. Higher risk if production in the basin declines.
  • Percent-of-Proceeds (POP): The midstream company keeps a percentage of the commodity (usually NGLs) as payment. This exposes the midstream firm to commodity price risk.

Refining Economics (Crack Spreads)

Refiners earn a margin based on the difference between the cost of crude oil and the price of refined products (gasoline/diesel). This is the Crack Spread.

3-2-1 Crack Spread: A proxy for refining margins assuming 3 barrels of crude produce 2 barrels of gasoline and 1 barrel of distillates. Margin = (2 × Gasoline Price + 1 × Distillate Price - 3 × Crude Price) / 3.

Renewable Project Finance & Tax Equity

The Partnership Flip Structure

  • Initial Phase: The Tax Equity investor receives the majority of income and tax benefits (often 99%) until they achieve a target IRR.
  • The Flip: Once the target yield is reached, the allocation "flips." The Developer then receives the majority of cash flows (e.g., 95%) and the Tax Equity investor retains a small residual stake.

Levelized Cost of Energy (LCOE)

LCOE = (PV of Capex + PV of O&M) / PV of Total Generation

💡 Key Insight: LCOE measures the cost to produce but ignores the value of that power. Solar LCOE might be low, but if it only produces at noon when power prices are negative, its economic value is poor. The relevant metric is the "Value Factor" — the average price received relative to the average market price.

Midstream & MLP Valuation

The MLP Structure

  • Pass-Through: MLPs pay no corporate income tax. Instead, they pass tax liability to "Unitholders" (shareholders).
  • Distributions: They pay out nearly all available cash flow as distributions (dividends).
  • IDRs (Incentive Distribution Rights): Historically, the General Partner (GP) managed the MLP and took a cut of the distributions. As distributions grew, the GP's cut increased (up to 50%), acting as a powerful incentive for growth but a major drag on the MLP's cost of capital.

Distributable Cash Flow (DCF)

Formula: Adjusted EBITDA - Interest - Taxes — Maintenance CAPEX

  • Coverage Ratio: DCF / Total Distributions Paid. A ratio of 1.1x - 1.2x is considered safe.
  • Valuation: MLPs are valued on Target Yield (e.g., Investor demands an 8% yield) or Price / DCF.

Risk Management & Hedging

  • Swaps: Fixes the price. Company receives fixed, pays floating. Locks in certainty but removes upside.
  • Collars: Buying a put (floor) and selling a call (ceiling). Protects the downside while capping the upside, usually at zero initial cost ("costless collar").
  • Basis Risk: The risk that the price at the local hub deviates from the benchmark. Companies must hedge this "basis differential" separately.
