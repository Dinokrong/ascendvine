Restructuring & Distressed Situations

Restructuring investment banking is a specialized field focused on advising companies (and their creditors) that are facing significant financial hurdles. Unlike traditional M&A or IPOs — which are fueled by optimism and growth — restructuring is driven by necessity, survival, and the legal frameworks governing bankruptcy. Restructuring involves significantly modifying the debt, operations, or structure of a company to address financial distress. The goal is usually to avoid liquidation and allow the company to continue operating as a "going concern."

Basic — Why Companies Need Restructuring

Companies enter restructuring when they cannot meet their financial obligations. This usually stems from two main issues:

  • Liquidity Crisis: The company lacks the cash to make immediate payments (interest, payroll, suppliers), even if its long-term assets are valuable.
  • Solvency Crisis: The company's total liabilities exceed its total assets. The equity value is effectively zero (or negative).
💡 Interview Tip: If asked why a company goes bankrupt, don't just say "bad earnings." Be specific: "A company fails when it cannot meet its debt obligations due to a liquidity crunch (running out of cash) or insolvency (liabilities > assets), often triggered by a missed interest payment or a breached covenant."

Common Causes of Distress

  • Macro Factors: Large-scale economic shifts such as a recession, a sudden spike in commodity prices, or a global pandemic.
  • Industry Disruption: Structural changes where a company's core product becomes obsolete (e.g., physical movie rentals vs. streaming).
  • Failed Leveraged Buyouts (LBOs): A common cause in finance. If a Private Equity firm burdens a company with too much debt based on optimistic projections that don't materialize, the company may be unable to service its interest payments.
  • Operational Mismanagement: Poor expansion strategies, bloated cost structures, or supply chain failures.

Signs of Distress & Monitoring

  • Market Signals: Bonds trading significantly below par (e.g., below 80 cents on the dollar) or widening Credit Default Swap (CDS) spreads indicate the market fears a default.
  • Credit Ratings: Downgrades by agencies like Moody's or S&P (e.g., falling from B to CCC) signal increasing risk of non-payment.
  • Financial Ratios: High leverage (Debt/EBITDA > 5x–6x) and low Interest Coverage (EBITDA/Interest < 1.5x) are classic red flags.
  • Accounting Red Flags: Stretching Payables (sudden increase in DPO suggests the company is delaying payments to suppliers), Maxed Revolver (fully drawing down the Revolving Credit Facility often signals desperation for cash).
💡 Key Insight: A company can have a high stock price but a poor credit rating (e.g., a high-growth tech company). Equity investors focus on future growth potential, while credit rating agencies focus on the stability of cash flows to service debt.

The Capital Structure & Priority of Claims

This hierarchy is governed by the Absolute Priority Rule (APR): senior claims must be paid in full before junior claims receive anything.

  1. Super-Senior Claims: Usually "DIP Financing" (Debtor-in-Possession loans provided during bankruptcy to keep the lights on) and administrative expenses (lawyers, bankers).
  2. Secured Debt: Bank Debt / Revolvers / Term Loan A (secured by "hard" assets like inventory, receivables, or PP&E). Term Loan B.
  3. Priority Unsecured Claims: Unpaid employee wages (up to a limit) and owed taxes.
  4. General Unsecured Claims (GUCs): Senior Notes (Bonds), Trade Payables (money owed to vendors).
  5. Subordinated Debt (Mezzanine): Junior debt that agrees to be paid after senior debt in exchange for higher interest rates.
  6. Equity: The owners (Common and Preferred Stock). In a restructuring, equity holders are essentially "out of the money" and often get wiped out completely.
💡 Key Insight: In a restructuring, value breaks at the Fulcrum Security. The fulcrum security is the most junior part of the capital structure that will receive some recovery, but likely not full recovery. The creditors holding this security often become the new owners of the reorganized company because the old equity is wiped out.

Default: How It Starts

  • Payment Default: The company misses a scheduled interest or principal payment. This is the most serious form.
  • Technical Default (Covenant Breach): The company violates a rule in its credit agreement (e.g., "Total Leverage must be < 4.0x"). The company hasn't run out of cash yet, but the lenders now have the right to accelerate the debt (demand full repayment immediately), forcing a restructuring conversation.

The Restructuring Toolkit

A. Out-of-Court Restructuring

The company negotiates with creditors to change the terms of debt without going to court.

  • Pros: Cheaper, faster, preserves privacy, avoids the "stigma" of bankruptcy.
  • Cons: Requires consensus. "Holdout" creditors can block the deal because you typically need 100% agreement to change principal/interest terms outside of bankruptcy.
  • Common Tactics: Amend and Extend (pushing back maturity dates in exchange for a higher interest rate or fees), Bondholder Haircut (convincing creditors to reduce the principal amount owed), Debt-for-Equity Swap (creditors trade their debt claims for ownership stakes in the company).

B. Chapter 11 Bankruptcy (Reorganization)

A court-supervised process where the company continues to operate while freezing creditor claims.

  • Automatic Stay: Upon filing, an "Automatic Stay" prevents creditors from collecting debts, seizing assets, or continuing lawsuits immediately. This gives the debtor "breathing room."
  • Debtor-in-Possession (DIP): The company remains in control of its operations.
  • Voting: The company proposes a Plan of Reorganization (POR). Creditors vote on it. The court can confirm the plan even if some creditors object (a "Cramdown"), provided strict legal tests are met.

C. Chapter 7 Bankruptcy (Liquidation)

The company stops operations. A trustee is appointed to sell off all assets piece-by-piece. Proceeds are distributed according to the Absolute Priority Rule, and the company ceases to exist. This is the "death" of the company.

💡 Interview Tip: You will often be asked the difference between Chapter 7 and Chapter 11. Chapter 7: Liquidation (Selling for parts). Management is removed; a trustee takes over. Chapter 11: Reorganization (Fixing the balance sheet to survive). Management usually stays.

Key Concepts

  • DIP Financing: Special super-priority debt raised during Chapter 11 to fund operations. It effectively jumps to the top of the capital structure.
  • Pre-Packaged Bankruptcy ("Pre-Pack"): The company negotiates a plan with creditors before filing for Chapter 11. They file, get swift court approval, and exit quickly (often in 30–90 days).
  • Section 363 Sale: A faster asset sale process within Chapter 11 that allows assets to be sold "free and clear" of liens.
  • Free-Fall Bankruptcy: The opposite of a Pre-Pack. The company files for Chapter 11 without a plan in place, leading to a longer, more expensive, and uncertain process.

The Banker's Role & 13-Week Cash Flow Model

Debtor Side (Company)

Goal: Survival. They want to cut debt, extend maturity dates, and maintain as much value for equity holders as possible. Work: Managing cash flow forecasts (13-week cash flows), negotiating with lenders, raising DIP financing.

Creditor Side (Lenders/Bondholders)

Client: Hedge funds, asset managers, or banks holding the debt. Typically organized as an "Ad Hoc Group" or an official "Unsecured Creditors Committee (UCC)." Goal: Maximizing Recovery. Work: Analyzing the company's true value, arguing for a larger share of the new company ("NewCo") equity, or pushing for liquidation if the business is flawed.

The 13-Week Cash Flow Model (TWCF)

In distress, cash is oxygen, and it must be monitored weekly. The TWCF uses the Direct Method of cash flow (Cash Receipts minus Cash Disbursements), not the Indirect Method used in standard accounting. It determines the need for DIP financing — if the model shows cash going negative in Week 6, the company needs a loan before then.

Advanced — Capital Structure Dynamics

Structural vs. Contractual Subordination

  • Contractual Subordination: This is defined within the credit agreement or indenture. A subordinated note explicitly agrees to be paid after senior debt within the same legal entity.
  • Structural Subordination: This arises from the corporate legal structure. If a Parent Company issues debt (High Yield Bonds) but the operating assets and cash flows are held at an Operating Subsidiary (OpCo) which has its own debt (Bank Debt), the Parent's debt is structurally subordinated to the OpCo's debt. The OpCo debt has a direct claim on the assets.
💡 Interview Tip: If asked, "Is secured debt always senior to unsecured debt?" the advanced answer is "Generally yes, but strictly speaking, priority is determined by the specific collateral package and corporate structure. Unsecured debt at an OpCo may effectively be senior to Secured debt at a HoldCo if the HoldCo's collateral is merely the stock of the OpCo."

Intercompany Loans and Guarantees

Lenders to a Parent company will often require Upstream Guarantees from the operating subsidiaries to mitigate structural subordination. This effectively gives the Parent lenders a direct claim against the subsidiary's assets. Courts may apply Equitable Subordination (pushing the claim to the back of the line) if an insider leveraged their position to the detriment of outside creditors.

Advanced — The Fulcrum Security & Valuation Warfare

Identifying the Fulcrum

Consider a capital structure: Senior Secured: $100M, Senior Unsecured: $100M, Subordinated Notes: $100M, Equity. If the Enterprise Value is estimated at $150M:

  • Senior Secured is covered 100% ($100M).
  • $50M of value remains.
  • Senior Unsecured is owed $100M but there is only $50M left.
  • Subordinated Notes and Equity are "out of the money" (OTM).

The Senior Unsecured creditors are the Fulcrum Class — they are likely to become the new owners of the reorganized company.

Valuation Warfare

  • Senior Creditors (Secured) often argue for a lower valuation. If the valuation is low, junior classes appear OTM and can be wiped out, leaving the seniors with 100% of the new equity.
  • Junior Creditors (Fulcrum/Subordinated) argue for a higher valuation. If the valuation is high enough, it proves there is value flowing down to them, entitling them to a recovery.
💡 Key Insight: The Fulcrum Security changes as valuation assumptions change. A 1.0x turn in EBITDA multiple can shift the fulcrum from the Unsecured Bonds to the Subordinated Notes. This sensitivity is why valuation testimony in court is so contentious.

Advanced — DIP Financing & Cash Collateral

Cash Collateral

Cash generated by the business (e.g., from collecting receivables) is usually encumbered by the pre-petition secured lenders' liens. The company cannot spend this "Cash Collateral" without the secured lenders' consent or a court order. To use cash collateral, the debtor often grants "Replacement Liens" on new assets generated post-petition to the old lenders.

DIP Financing

  • Super-Priority Administrative Claim: DIP lenders get paid before almost everyone else.
  • Priming Liens: In aggressive scenarios, a DIP loan can be secured by a "priming lien," meaning it takes priority over existing pre-petition secured liens. This is highly controversial and requires showing that pre-petition lenders are "adequately protected."
  • Roll-Up DIP: A common tactic where an existing lender provides the DIP loan on the condition that the proceeds are used to pay off their old (Pre-Petition) debt. This "rolls up" their old debt into the new, safer, super-priority DIP facility.

Advanced — Plan Confirmation & The Cramdown

A Chapter 11 case concludes when a Plan of Reorganization (POR) is confirmed. A class is deemed to accept the plan if: more than 1/2 in number of creditors who vote accept (Numerosity) AND at least 2/3 in dollar amount of claims that vote accept (Magnitude).

The "Cramdown"

If a class votes to reject the plan, the debtor can attempt a "Cramdown" (confirming the plan over the objection of a dissenting class). To cram down a plan, two main tests must be met under Section 1129(b):

  • No Unfair Discrimination: A dissenting class cannot be treated significantly worse than a class of equal rank.
  • Fair and Equitable Test (Absolute Priority Rule): A junior class cannot receive anything unless all senior dissenting classes are paid in full. If Unsecured Creditors vote NO and are not paid in full, the Equity holders cannot retain any ownership in the reorganized company.

The "New Value" Exception

Old equity holders can sometimes retain equity even if creditors aren't paid in full, provided they contribute "New Value" (new capital) that is substantial, necessary, and in the form of money or money's worth.

Advanced — Distressed M&A & Legal Maneuvers

Section 363 Sales: The Stalking Horse Bidder

To avoid a "fire sale" atmosphere, the debtor usually lines up an initial bidder called the Stalking Horse. The Stalking Horse sets the floor price and negotiates the Asset Purchase Agreement (APA). To compensate them for the time and risk of setting the floor, they receive Bid Protections, such as a Break-Up Fee (1–3% of deal value) and Expense Reimbursement if they are outbid at the auction.

Credit Bidding

Secured creditors have a powerful right called Credit Bidding. They can "bid" the amount of debt they are owed as currency in the auction for the assets securing that debt. This prevents assets from being sold for a low price that forces the lender to take a loss. It allows lenders to "loan-to-own."

Legal Maneuvers

  • Voidable Preferences (The "90-Day Rule"): A "Preference" occurs if the company pays a specific creditor in the 90 days before filing, giving them more than they would have received in Chapter 7. The Trustee can sue to "claw back" this money.
  • Fraudulent Conveyance: Occurs if a company transfers assets for less than fair value while insolvent. The court can void the transaction and return the asset to the estate.
  • Critical Vendor Motions: A debtor can file a "Critical Vendor Motion" to pay specific suppliers immediately if their goods are essential to keeping the business running.

Advanced — Distressed Investing & Liability Management

Distressed Investing Strategies

  • Loan-to-Own: An investor buys secured debt at a discount (e.g., 60 cents), intending to credit bid that debt in a 363 sale or swap it for equity in Chapter 11. If successful, they acquire the company at a low effective valuation.
  • Blocking Positions: An investor buys just enough debt (e.g., 34% of a class) to block a plan of reorganization (since you need 2/3 approval). They use this "blocking position" to hold out for a better deal or "Greenmail."
  • Basis Convergence: Buying bonds at a discount and hedging with CDS or equity shorts, betting that the gap between the instruments will close as the restructuring plays out.

Out-of-Court Liability Management Exercises (LMEs)

  • Dropdown (J.Crew) Transactions: The company uses a "basket" in its credit agreement to transfer valuable assets (like IP) into an "Unrestricted Subsidiary" not bound by the credit agreement's covenants. Once the assets are in the Unrestricted Sub, the company can borrow new debt against those assets, stripping collateral away from existing lenders.
  • Uptiering (Serta) Transactions: A group of lenders (often a majority 50.1%) agrees to amend the credit documents to allow the company to issue new "Super-Priority" debt. The participating lenders exchange their old debt for this new Super-Priority debt, pushing the non-participating minority lenders down the priority ladder. This is often called "Lender-on-Lender violence."
