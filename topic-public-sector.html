Public Sector & Infrastructure (PSI)

The Public Sector & Infrastructure (PSI) vertical covers essential physical systems and facilities that support economic activity. Investment banking in this sector involves financing, advising, and structuring deals for airports, toll roads, energy grids, water systems, hospitals, schools, and digital infrastructure. The key distinction from corporate finance is the use of Project Finance — where debt is tied to the asset's cash flows, not the sponsor's balance sheet.

Basic — Infrastructure vs. Corporate Finance

The Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV)

  • Ring-Fencing: The asset is legally isolated from the parent company. If the project goes bankrupt, lenders cannot pursue the parent company's other assets.
  • Non-Recourse Debt: Because of this isolation, debt is "non-recourse." Lenders can only claim the assets and cash flows of the specific project, not the sponsor's balance sheet.

Modeling Horizon

  • Corporate Finance: Forecasts typically span 3-5 years before assuming a terminal value.
  • Infrastructure: Models often span 20 to 50+ years. Because contracts (concessions) are long-term and assets have finite lives, analysts model the cash flows explicitly until the end of the asset's life, without a terminal value.

Infrastructure Subsectors

Transportation Infrastructure

  • Airports: Runways, terminals, and retail operations. Revenue comes from airline landing fees (aeronautical) and parking/shopping (non-aeronautical).
  • Ports: Container terminals and bulk cargo facilities. Highly correlated with global trade volumes.
  • Toll Roads & Bridges: Highways or crossings where users pay a fee. Can be "Greenfield" (new construction) or "Brownfield" (existing operational asset).

Social Infrastructure

These assets provide social services to the community. They are typically backed by government contracts rather than direct user fees.

  • Revenue Model: Usually based on Availability Payments. The government pays the operator a fixed fee as long as the facility is open, clean, and functional, regardless of how many people use it.

Digital Infrastructure

  • Data Centers: Facilities housing servers and networking equipment.
  • Fiber Optic Networks: Cables transmitting data.
  • Cell Towers: Vertical structures hosting wireless equipment.

Business Models & Contract Structures

The Concession Model

  • Definition: A contract between a public authority (government) and a private company (concessionaire) granting the right to operate an asset for a specific period (e.g., 30, 50, or 99 years).
  • End of Life: At the end of the concession, ownership of the asset typically reverts to the government for free.

Demand Risk vs. Availability Risk

  • Demand Risk (Volume Risk): The operator takes the risk that traffic/usage will be lower than expected (e.g., Toll Roads). Higher risk, higher return.
  • Availability Risk: The operator is paid by the government as long as the asset is available for use (e.g., Hospitals). Lower risk, lower return.

Key Metrics for Infrastructure

The Cash Flow Waterfall

  1. Revenues flow in.
  2. Opex (Operating Expenses) is paid first.
  3. Taxes are paid.
  4. Debt Service (interest + Principal) is paid next.
  5. Reserve Accounts (e.g., Maintenance Reserve) are funded.
  6. Equity Distributions: Only what is left at the bottom flows to investors.

CFADS (Cash Flow Available for Debt Service)

CFADS = EBITDA - Cash Taxes — Maintenance CapEx +/- Working Capital Changes

Unlike corporate finance where lenders might look at Debt/EBITDA, infrastructure lenders look at CFADS / Debt Service (the DSCR).

Valuation Considerations

  • Discounted Cash Flow (DCF): The gold standard for infrastructure, but with a finite life: unlike a corporate DCF with a perpetual Terminal Value, a concession DCF runs only until the end of the contract period.
  • Terminal Value: Often zero (asset reverts to state) or a small scrap value.
  • Regulated Asset Base (RAB) Multiples: EV/RAB. A ratio > 1.0x (premium to RAB) implies the market believes the regulator will allow attractive returns or that the company can outperform regulatory efficiency targets.

Industry Dynamics: Greenfield vs. Brownfield

  • Greenfield (Construction Phase): High risk. You must model the construction loan drawdowns, capitalized interest, and potential delays. Returns must be high (Equity IRR 12-15%+).
  • Brownfield (Operational Phase): Lower risk. The asset is built and generating cash. Valuation is about optimizing the capital structure and operational efficiency. Returns are lower (Equity IRR 7-10%).

Advanced — Finite-Life DCF & Concession Valuation

  • No Perpetuity: The Terminal Value (TV) is often zero, a scrap value, or a contractually defined "handback" payment.
  • Handback Liabilities: Models must account for specific "handback requirements" (e.g., road quality standards) that trigger significant capex in the final years of the concession, often creating negative cash flows near the end of the model.
  • Exit Multiple Compression: If you model a sale in Year 10 of a 30-year concession, the Exit Multiple must be lower than the Entry Multiple. Because the asset in Year 10 has 20 years of life remaining, it is worth less than the same asset on day 1 with 30 years remaining. Failing to account for this compression is a classic modeling error.
💡 Interview Tip: If asked how to value a toll road with 30 years remaining, never mention "Terminal Value based on Gordon Growth." Instead, describe a "Run-off DCF" where cash flows are projected explicitly for 30 years with zero terminal value, and the discount rate reflects the specific risk profile (demand risk, regulatory risk, country risk).

Advanced Debt Modeling: Sizing & Sculpting

Debt Sizing via Coverage Ratios

  • The Logic: "We will lend you exactly enough money so that even in your lowest cash flow year, you still have 30% cushion."
  • Sculpting: Instead of equal principal payments (linear amortization), principal payments are adjusted ("sculpted") up or down each year. In high revenue years, you pay more principal. In low revenue years, you pay less. The goal is to maintain a constant DSCR throughout the loan life.

Greenfield Development Modeling

The Construction Loan

  • Drawdowns: You don't borrow the full amount on Day 1. You "draw down" debt monthly as you pay construction contractors.
  • IDC (Interest During Construction): During construction, there is no revenue to pay interest. Therefore, interest is capitalized (added to the loan balance).
  • Refinancing: Once construction is complete and the asset is operating ("COD" - Commercial Operations Date), the risky Construction Loan is usually repaid by drawing down a cheaper, long-term "Permanent Financing" facility.

The J-Curve Effect

  • Years 1-3: Large negative cash outflows (capital calls).
  • Years 4-6: Low/volatile inflows (ramp-up).
  • Years 7+: Stable inflows. Valuation is highly sensitive to the discount rate applied to those early negative years.

Advanced Metrics & Covenants

DSCR vs. LLCR

  • DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio): CFADS / Debt Service. Measures liquidity (can I pay today?). Used for "Lock-up" covenants (stopping dividends).
  • LLCR (Loan Life Coverage Ratio): NPV(CFADS over loan life) / Outstanding Debt. Measures solvency (is there enough total value to repay the loan?). Used for debt sizing and refinancing.
💡 Common Mistake: Confusing EBITDA with CFADS. Infrastructure assets have heavy maintenance capex (e.g., resurfacing a road). EBITDA ignores this; CFADS captures it. Lenders lend against CFADS, not EBITDA. Always adjust for maintenance capex when sizing infrastructure debt.

Infrastructure M&A Considerations

Refinancing Gain Sharing

  • In PPPs, if the sponsor refinances the debt at a lower rate after the construction phase (when risk drops), the resulting interest savings increase equity IRR.
  • Clawback: Most government contracts require the sponsor to share 50-70% of this "refinancing gain" with the public sector. Models must account for this leakage.

Consortium Dynamics

  • Bids often involve a consortium (e.g., a Construction Company + an Infrastructure Fund).
  • Construction Co: Wants high construction margins, low long-term equity risk.
  • Infra Fund: Wants low construction price, high long-term yield.
  • Conflict: During the bid, these interests clash. The financial model acts as the "truth" to align incentives.
