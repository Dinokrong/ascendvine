Equity Capital Markets (ECM)

Equity Capital Markets (ECM) sits at the intersection of investment banking and sales & trading. While an M&A banker focuses on buying and selling entire companies, an ECM banker focuses on buying and selling pieces of companies — specifically, equity securities. In ECM, questions often test your understanding of process and market dynamics rather than just hard accounting. Focus on understanding why a company issues equity and how supply and demand dictate pricing.

Basic — The Lifecycle of Equity Financing

Before a company reaches the IPO stage, it typically passes through several stages of private financing. Understanding this "capital ladder" is crucial because the terms set here dictate the capital structure at the IPO.

  • Stage 1 — Bootstrapping & Angel Investors: Initially funded by the founder's own cash. Angel Investors are high-net-worth individuals who write smaller checks (e.g., $50k–$500k) for very early, high-risk equity.
  • Stage 2 — Venture Capital (Series A, B, C): Once the company has a product and initial revenue, it raises institutional capital in "Rounds." Series A focuses on product-market fit; Series B/C focus on scaling. VC funds operate on a "power law" model, assuming one or two massive winners will return the entire fund.
  • Stage 3 — Growth Equity: Between late-stage VC and an IPO. Unlike VCs who take risks on unproven technology, Growth Equity firms invest in proven, high-growth companies that need capital to expand.

The Function of Equity Capital Markets

At its core, ECM is responsible for originating and executing equity capital raises. This group advises on:

  • Sizing: How much capital to raise without diluting existing owners too heavily.
  • Structure: Common stock vs. Convertible bonds vs. Preferred stock.
  • Timing: Launching when market volatility is low and investor appetite is high.
  • Pricing: Balancing the issuer's desire for max cash vs. the investor's desire for a return.

Primary vs. Secondary Markets

  • Primary Market: New securities are created and sold for the first time. Cash flows from Investors → The Company (Issuer). Creates dilution because the total share count increases.
  • Secondary Market: Existing securities are traded among investors. Cash flows from Investor → Investor. Generally no dilution as shares are just changing hands.
💡 Key Insight: When a Private Equity firm takes a portfolio company public, they often sell their own shares ("cashing out") rather than just having the company issue new shares. This is a Secondary Offering within an IPO. The company gets no cash from those specific shares sold by the PE firm.

Common vs. Preferred Stock

In the private markets (VC/Growth), investors typically buy Preferred Stock, while in the public markets (IPO), investors buy Common Stock.

  • Common Stock: Standard ownership. Includes voting rights (usually) but is last in line during a liquidation.
  • Preferred Stock: A hybrid security. It sits above common stock in the capital stack.
    • Liquidation Preference: In a sale or bankruptcy, preferred holders get their money back (often 1x their investment) before common shareholders get a dime.
    • Dividends: Often carries a fixed dividend.
    • Conversion: Typically converts into Common Stock at the IPO.

The Initial Public Offering (IPO)

An IPO is the transformational event where a private company sells shares to the public for the first time and lists on a stock exchange (like the NYSE or Nasdaq).

Why Go Public?

  • Access to Capital: Efficient way to raise massive amounts of capital.
  • Liquidity: Allows early investors (Founders, VCs, PE) to monetize their stakes.
  • Currency: Public stock can be used to acquire other companies (M&A).
  • Prestige: "Stamp of approval" for customers and vendors.

The Downsides

  • Disclosure: Requires revealing margins and strategy to competitors via filings (10-K, 10-Q).
  • Short-termism: Pressure to hit quarterly earnings targets.
  • Cost: Compliance (Sarbanes-Oxley), listing fees, and investor relations.

The IPO Process Timeline

If asked to "Walk me through an IPO," focus on the commercial milestones. The process generally takes 4–6 months.

  1. Phase 1 — The Bake-off (Selection): Investment banks pitch the company to win the mandate. The "Lead Left Bookrunner" is the bank that runs the process, holds the pen on the prospectus, and manages the stabilization.
  2. Phase 2 — Due Diligence & Filing (The S-1): Bankers and lawyers draft the S-1 Registration Statement. The "Red Herring" is the preliminary prospectus circulated to investors before the price is finalized. The SEC reviews the S-1 and provides comments that must be addressed before the deal can launch.
  3. Phase 3 — The Roadshow (Marketing): Management travels to financial hubs to pitch institutional investors (mutual funds, hedge funds). Investors submit "indications of interest" (IOIs) stating how many shares they want and at what price (Bookbuilding).
  4. Phase 4 — Pricing & Allocation: The night before trading, the bankers price the deal based on the order book. If demand exceeds supply (e.g., 5x covered), bankers can price at the top of the range.
  5. Phase 5 — Trading & Stabilization: The stock opens for trading. The Lead Left bank actively manages the initial trading to ensure stability using the Greenshoe.

The Syndicate Structure & The Greenshoe

  • Lead Left Bookrunner: The quarterback. Runs the model, the prospectus, and stabilization.
  • Joint Bookrunners: Major banks that help market the deal and provide valuation input.
  • Co-Managers: Smaller banks that help with distribution but have little strategic input.
💡 Interview Tip: The difference between a "Bookrunner" and a "Co-Manager" is risk and work. Bookrunners underwrite the risk and build the book. Co-managers are essentially sales agents.

The "Greenshoe" (Overallotment Option)

The Greenshoe Option allows underwriters to sell up to 15% more shares than originally planned to stabilize the price.

  • Overselling: The bank intentionally sells 115% of the deal size. They are now "short" 15%.
  • Scenario A (Stock Rises): The bank exercises the option, buying the extra 15% from the company at the IPO price. The company raises more cash.
  • Scenario B (Stock Falls): The bank buys shares in the open market to cover their short. This buying pressure supports the stock price.

Follow-On Offerings & Dilution Math

  • Follow-On Offering (FPO): Issuing more stock post-IPO. Dilutive if new shares are created (Primary). Non-Dilutive if existing shareholders sell (Secondary).
  • Shelf Registration (Rule 415): A company files a "Shelf" registration statement (Form S-3) that allows them to issue securities periodically over a 3-year window without re-filing for every single transaction.

Pre-Money vs. Post-Money

Post-Money Valuation = Pre-Money Valuation + New Cash Raised

  • Up Round: The new share price is higher than the previous round. Early investors see a "paper gain."
  • Down Round: The new share price is lower than the previous round. This is dangerous and can trigger "anti-dilution" clauses that severely hurt the founders.

Advanced — IPO Mechanics: Stabilization and The Greenshoe

A standard Greenshoe option allows underwriters to sell up to 15% more shares than the original deal size at the IPO price. This option is valid for 30 days.

  • The Naked Short: When the IPO allocates, the underwriters intentionally over-allocate the deal. If the deal size is 10M shares, they allocate 11.5M shares (115%). The bank is now "short" 1.5M shares.
  • Scenario A (Stock Pops): If the stock trades at $25 (IPO Price: $20), the underwriters exercise the Greenshoe. They buy the 1.5M shares from the issuer at $20 and deliver them to investors. The issuer effectively raises 15% more capital.
  • Scenario B (Stock Breaks): If the stock trades at $18, the underwriters execute a Stabilizing Bid. They buy 1.5M shares in the open market at $18, creating artificial demand to support the price floor. The underwriters make a trading profit.

Lock-Up Agreements & Early Release

While typically 180 days, modern lock-ups often have Early Release Triggers. Example: "If the stock trades at >133% of the IPO price for 10 trading days, 20% of locked-up shares are released immediately." This helps smooth out the supply shock.

Advanced — The "IPO Discount" Logic

Investors demand a discount (typically 10–15% vs. peers) to compensate for:

  • Liquidity Risk: No trading history.
  • Information Asymmetry: Management knows more than investors.
  • The "Pop": A 15% pop is engineered to generate marketing buzz and goodwill. A flat IPO is viewed as a failure, while a huge pop (+80%) implies the company "left money on the table."

Advanced — Rights Issues and Dilution Math

For distressed companies or those in Europe/UK, Rights Issues are common. Existing shareholders are given the "right" to buy new shares at a discount to avoid dilution.

Theoretical Ex-Rights Price (TERP)

TERP = ((Old Shares × Old Price) + (New Shares × Offer Price)) / Total Shares

Example: Old: 100M shares @ $20 ($2B Market Cap). Raise: $200M @ $10/share (20M new shares). TERP = ($2000M + $200M) / 120M = $18.33

Value of the Right

The "Right" itself is tradeable. Value of Right = TERP - Offer Price = $18.33 - $10.00 = $8.33

💡 Key Insight: A shareholder who exercises their rights suffers zero economic loss. The drop in share price is perfectly offset by the gain on the cheap new shares. If they do not exercise, they get massively diluted.

Advanced — Anti-Dilution Provisions

In private markets (Down Rounds), anti-dilution clauses protect investors.

  • Full Ratchet: The most aggressive protection. If the company issues any new shares at a lower price, the early investor's conversion price is repriced to that new, lower price — as if they had invested at that low price all along. This is brutal for founders.
  • Weighted Average: The market standard. The conversion price is adjusted based on a formula that considers both the new price and the number of shares issued. It is a "blended" adjustment, far less punitive than a Full Ratchet.

Advanced — Alternative Listing Methods: SPACs and Direct Listings

Direct Listings (DPO)

  • No New Capital: Traditionally just a liquidity event for existing holders.
  • Reference Price: The exchange sets a reference price, but the opening trade is purely supply/demand. No bankers "setting" the price.
  • No Lock-up: Insiders can typically sell immediately.

SPACs (Special Purpose Acquisition Companies)

A "Blank Check" company IPOs to raise cash to buy a private target.

  • The Trust Account: IPO proceeds are held in a trust (invested in Treasuries) while the Sponsor hunts for a target (typically 18–24 months).
  • The Promote: Sponsors typically get 20% of the equity for nominal consideration. This is the "cost" of the SPAC structure and is highly dilutive.
  • Redemption Rights: Before the merger (De-SPAC), shareholders can vote "no" and redeem their shares for their original $10 + interest.
  • Warrants: SPAC units often come with warrants (kickers) to entice hedge funds to park cash in the trust.

Advanced — PIPE Transactions & Convertible Bonds

PIPE (Private Investment in Public Equity)

A PIPE involves selling public shares to private investors at a discount. Often used in SPACs to validate the deal valuation and replace capital lost to redemptions, or by distressed companies needing fast cash without a roadshow. Executed quickly; investors demand a discount for the illiquidity and speed.

Convertible Bonds (Equity Perspective)

ECM bankers pitch Convertibles ("CBs") to lower the cost of capital. A Convertible Bond is a hybrid:

  • Bond Floor: The value of the security as pure debt (PV of coupons + principal). Even if the stock crashes, the bond is worth this floor.
  • Option Value: The value of the call option on the stock.
  • Lower Coupon: The issuer pays less interest (e.g., 1%) than straight debt (e.g., 6%) because investors value the equity option.
  • Delayed Dilution: The conversion price is set at a premium (e.g., 30% above today's price). If converted, the company effectively sells equity at a higher price than it could today.
  • Convertible Arbitrage: Hedge funds buy the CB and short the stock to hedge the delta. They profit from the yield and the volatility (gamma), not the stock direction.
