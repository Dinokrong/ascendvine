Media and Telecommunications (TMT)

The Media and Telecommunications (TMT) sector represents a cornerstone of the modern global economy, encompassing the infrastructure that connects the world and the content that informs and entertains it. Often the largest and most active coverage group at major banks, TMT offers exposure to household names and fast-paced innovation.

Basic — Media & Telecom Subsectors

Telecommunications (Telecom)

  • Wireless Services: Companies providing mobile voice and data services. This subsector is characterized by massive capital expenditures (CapEx) for spectrum licenses and network equipment. It is often an oligopoly (3-4 major players dominate most markets).
  • Wireline & Fiber: Providers of fixed-line internet and enterprise data solutions. Historically focused on copper landlines, the industry has shifted almost entirely to fiber-optic connectivity.
  • Cable & Broadband: Originally providers of television signals via coaxial cable, these companies have evolved into dominant high-speed internet providers (ISPs). They often operate as regional monopolies or duopolies.
  • Infrastructure (Towers & Satellites): Companies that own the physical assets — such as cell towers — and lease space to wireless carriers. They function almost like Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs) due to their stable, long-term lease structures.

Media & Entertainment

  • Content Studios: The creators of movies and television shows. Their economics are hit-driven and involve high upfront production costs with uncertain payoffs.
  • Networks & Broadcasting: Traditional TV channels (broadcast and cable) that aggregate content. They earn revenue through affiliate fees (paid by cable companies) and advertising.
  • Streaming / OTT (Over-The-Top): Direct-to-consumer platforms that bypass traditional cable distribution. These businesses focus on subscriber growth and retention.
  • Gaming: Interactive entertainment companies. This sector has shifted from selling physical discs (one-time revenue) to "Live Services" (recurring revenue via subscriptions and in-game microtransactions).

Business Models

The Subscription Model (Recurring)

  • Drivers: Pricing power (ARPU) and customer retention (Churn).
  • Examples: Wireless carriers, Cable ISPs, Netflix, Spotify.

OTT Pricing Models

  • SVOD (Subscription Video on Demand): Users pay a recurring monthly fee for unlimited access (e.g., Netflix, Disney+). The focus here is on reducing churn and acquiring new subs.
  • AVOD (Ad-Based Video on Demand): Content is free or discounted but includes commercials (e.g., Hulu with Ads, Peacock Free). Revenue is derived from advertisers paying for impressions.
  • TVOD (Transactional Video on Demand): Users pay per piece of content (rent or buy) (e.g., iTunes, Amazon Prime Video Store). This is a lower-margin, non-recurring model.

Affiliate Fees & Licensing

  • Affiliate Fees: A cable provider (e.g., Comcast) pays a cable network (e.g., ESPN) a monthly fee per subscriber to carry that channel. This is highly lucrative and stable revenue for the network.
  • Licensing/Syndication: A studio sells the rights to air a show to a network or streaming service for a set period. This is high-margin "rent" on intellectual property.

Key Operating Metrics

Telecom & Subscription Metrics

  • ARPU (Average Revenue Per User): Formula: Total Recurring Revenue / Average Subscribers. Measures monetization. Is the company upselling customers to higher tiers?
  • Churn Rate: Formula: Monthly Churn = Cancellations in Month / Beginning Subscribers. The inverse of retention. High churn destroys value because acquiring new customers is expensive.
  • SAC (Subscriber Acquisition Cost) / CPGA (Cost Per Gross Add): The marketing and commission costs required to acquire one new customer. Critical for calculating unit economics.
  • Net Adds: Formula: Gross Adds - Churned Subs. The absolute growth in the subscriber base.

Media & Engagement Metrics

  • DAU (Daily Active Users): Unique users who engage with the product daily.
  • MAU (Monthly Active Users): Unique users who engage at least once a month.
  • Stickiness (DAU/MAU Ratio): A 50% ratio means the average user opens the app 15 days a month. Social apps (Facebook/WhatsApp) aim for >50%.
  • CPM (Cost Per Mille): The cost an advertiser pays for 1,000 impressions (views) of an advertisement. Premium content (like Live Sports) commands high CPMs.

Valuation Considerations

EV/EBITDA

This is the primary valuation metric for 90% of mature TMT companies.

  • Why EBITDA? Telecom and Media companies have massive Depreciation & Amortization (D&A).
  • Telecom: Depreciation from billions spent on network infrastructure.
  • Media: Amortization of content production costs (movies/TV shows are capitalized and amortized).

EV/(EBITDA - CapEx)

  • Logic: EBITDA ignores Capital Expenditures. For a telecom carrier, "Maintenance CapEx" (keeping the network running) is a real cash cost that is mandatory.
  • Proxy for OpFCF: (EBITDA - CapEx) is a proxy for Operating Free Cash Flow. It penalizes companies that are inefficient with their capital spending.

Industry Dynamics

Network Effects

A key competitive advantage in media platforms (social media, search).

  • Definition: The value of the product increases for all users as more users join.
  • Example: Facebook is valuable because everyone is on it. A new competitor cannot just build a better app; they need to move the entire user base. This creates a "winner-take-most" dynamic and a massive moat against new entrants.

Cord-Cutting and Bundling

  • "Cord-cutting" refers to the trend of cancelling traditional Pay-TV packages in favor of streaming.
  • Unbundling: Streaming allowed consumers to pick and choose.
  • Re-bundling: Ironically, the industry is shifting back toward bundles (e.g., Disney+, Hulu, and ESPN+ sold together) to reduce churn and increase pricing power.

Advanced — Spectrum Valuation ($/MHz-Pop)

Formula: Value = Price per MHz-Pop × Bandwidth (MHz) × Population Covered (Pop)

  • Low-Band (Sub-1 GHz): "Beachfront" property. Excellent propagation and building penetration. Historically commands a premium for coverage.
  • Mid-Band (1-6 GHz): The sweet spot for 5G, balancing coverage and capacity.
  • High-Band (mmWave): Massive capacity but poor propagation; requires dense infrastructure.

Infrastructure Valuation (Towers, Fiber, Data Centers)

  • Tower Cash Flow (TCF): Often used instead of EBITDA. TCF = EBITDA — Maintenance CapEx.
  • Tenancy Ratio: The number of tenants per tower (colocation). Increasing the tenancy ratio from 1.0x to 2.0x generates massive incremental margins because the cost to add a second tenant is minimal.
  • Fiber Valuation: Often valued on EV/Fiber Mile or EV/Route Mile. Differentiates between "Lit Fiber" (active, carrying data) and "Dark Fiber" (unused capacity).
  • Data Centers: Valued on EV/MW (Megawatt) of power capacity or EV/SqFt, reflecting the critical constraint of power availability in the AI era.

Advanced Accounting: Content & FCF Conversion

The "Content Accounting" Trap

  • Income Statement: Content costs are capitalized and then amortized over the estimated life of the title. This "Content Amortization" is a non-cash expense that reduces Net Income and EBITDA.
  • Cash Flow Statement: The actual cash spent to produce the movie is an "Addition to Content Assets," typically found in Cash Flow from Operations (CFO) or Investing Activities.
  • The Divergence: For a growing streamer, Cash Spend > Amortization. They are producing new shows faster than old shows are amortizing. Result: EBITDA might look healthy (because it adds back amortization) but Free Cash Flow is deeply negative (because cash is being paid out).
💡 Key Insight: To calculate "Real" FCF for a streamer, do not just use EBITDA — CapEx. You must calculate EBITDA — Cash Content Spend - Taxes — Change in WC.

M&A and Strategic Considerations

Regulatory Hurdles: T-Mobile / Sprint Case Study

  • The merger of T-Mobile and Sprint (2020) reduced the US wireless market from 4 major players to 3.
  • Antitrust Concern: Higher prices for consumers due to reduced competition.
  • The Solution (The "Fix"): To get DOJ approval, they had to create a new 4th competitor. They divested assets (Boost Mobile, Spectrum) to DISH Network, positioning DISH to enter as a new carrier.
  • Strategic Rationale: T-Mobile needed Sprint's Mid-Band Spectrum (2.5 GHz) to build a leading 5G network. The spectrum synergy was the "crown jewel" of the deal.

The Conglomerate Discount & Vertical Integration

Why did AT&T fail with Time Warner?

  • The Thesis: Own the pipe (Distribution) and the content (Time Warner/HBO) to gain data synergies and pricing power.
  • Culture Clash: Utility culture (AT&T) vs. Creative culture (HBO).
  • Debt Load: Massive leverage restricted agility.
  • Result: AT&T spun off WarnerMedia (which merged with Discovery) to refocus on connectivity.
  • Lesson: Vertical integration is difficult to execute. The market often applies a "conglomerate discount" rather than a premium.
